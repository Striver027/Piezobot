# Piezobot

**A desktop-scale, low-cost stick–slip piezoelectric robot for validating
swarm-intelligence algorithms that require fine relative positioning.**

This repository contains the editable hardware design files and firmware that
accompany the HardwareX article on Piezobot. It is intended to be sufficient for
another group to reproduce the robot and reproduce the measurements reported in
the article.

---

## Overview

Piezobot is a small mobile robot whose motion is produced by a **stick–slip
(friction–inertia) mechanism**: two piezoelectric stacks drive the robot across a
flat ferromagnetic surface, while N52 permanent magnets simultaneously provide the
preload force and the friction surface. Driving the stacks with a sawtooth waveform
generates a small net displacement on every cycle, which accumulates into
centimetre-scale travel. Turning is obtained by switching off the drive signal of one
of the two channels.

The robot is built from **stacked PCBs**, so the mechanical structure requires almost
no fasteners and the boards can be substituted to suit different experiments. Its
small size (3.7 cm body diameter) allows a high density of individuals to be placed
within a single camera field of view, which is what makes it useful as a
swarm-robotics test platform.

## Key specifications

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Body diameter | 3.7 cm |
| Actuation | 2 × piezoelectric stacks, stick–slip (friction–inertia) |
| Preload / friction surface | N52 permanent magnets |
| Drive voltage | 30 V (single Li-ion cell + on-board boost converter) |
| Drive waveform | Sawtooth, 2 kHz, generated by timer-triggered DMA (open loop) |
| Measured travelling speed | ≈ 1.6 mm/s (16 mm in 10 s) |
| Net displacement per drive cycle | ≈ 0.8 µm |
| Steering | Differential — switching off one drive channel turns the robot |
| Position holding | Retained when the drive signal is removed |
| Communication | 433 MHz wireless module |
| Working surface | Flat, ferromagnetic (tests performed on 430 stainless steel) |

> Piezobot is an **open-loop, single-prototype** platform at this stage: displacement
> is estimated from the number of drive cycles, with no position feedback. Positioning
> error is intended to be compensated by the vision system of the swarm-validation
> platform.

## Hardware

Four boards are stacked, from bottom to top: **PZT → power → controller → function**. They
share a common power and signal interface. Above the stack, at the very top of the robot,
sits the **LCD board**: the 0.99-inch display is taped onto it, the display ribbon cable is
soldered to it, and it converts the display signals into an FPC interface that connects to
the controller board. Neither the DAP board nor the BAT board is part of the stack: the DAPLink-end adapter in the
DAP board panel is used only as an aid for flashing the firmware, and the BAT board is the
battery adapter that powers the robot.

| Board | Function |
|---|---|
| **PZT board** (bottom of the stack) | Carries and wires the piezoelectric stacks |
| **Power board** | Boost converter (3.7 V → 30 V) and power operational amplifier that generates the drive waveform |
| **Controller board** | STM32G431 main controller; two DAC channels generate the sawtooth waveforms; the FPC from the LCD board connects here |
| **Function board** (top of the stack) | Six-axis IMU and 433 MHz wireless module |
| **LCD board** (above the stack, topmost part of the robot) | Display base board: the 0.99-inch display is taped onto it and its ribbon cable is soldered to it; converts the display signals into an FPC interface |
| **DAP board** (outside the stack) | Panelized adapter (PowerLink 2.54 mm ↔ 0.8 mm BTB): one larger link-end adapter board plus two smaller boards (programming, two batteries); the link-end adapter is used to flash the firmware |
| **BAT board** (outside the stack) | Battery adapter with the same panel structure and layout as the DAP board, but with thickened lead connections, so the two are not interchangeable; used to retrofit the battery that powers the robot |

The PCB sources are **Altium Designer** files (`.PrjPcb`, `.SchDoc`, `.PcbDoc`);
PDF schematics and fabrication-ready Gerber/drill archives are provided alongside them
so that the boards can be reviewed and manufactured without Altium. See
[`Hardware/README.md`](Hardware/README.md) for the complete file inventory.

The mechanical design is provided in [`CAD/`](CAD) in two formats: the editable
Autodesk Fusion 360 sources (`Piezobot_Assembly.f3z`, `CNC/aluminium_base.f3d`) and the
same geometry as neutral STEP files (`Piezobot_Assembly.step`, `CNC/aluminium_base.step`).
The assembly contains all parts of the robot, including the CNC-machined aluminium base
and the 3D-printed shell and LCD retaining ring; the parts to be printed are also
provided as STL. See [`CAD/README.md`](CAD/README.md) for the file inventory.

## Firmware

The firmware runs on an STM32G4-series microcontroller. It generates two 12-bit
sawtooth waveforms through the internal DACs, which the power board amplifies to 30 V
to drive the piezoelectric stacks. The waveform is produced by a timer triggering DMA
transfers, and the travelling speed is controlled by changing the driving frequency
(within the tested range, speed is approximately proportional to frequency).

**Firmware source code: `firmware/` — _to be added before the v1.0 release._**

## Getting started

1. Assemble the robot following the article (see *How to cite* below). Details of the
   assembly, including the magnet orientation, the glue points and the contact
   resistance check between the brass screw and the base, are given in the article's
   build instructions.
2. Charge/connect the single Li-ion cell.
3. Place the robot on a flat ferromagnetic surface (the article reports measurements on
   a 430 stainless steel plate).
4. Power the robot: the controller starts generating the 2 kHz sawtooth waveform and
   the robot begins stepping.
5. To change speed, change the driving frequency. To turn, disable the drive signal on
   one of the two channels.
6. Remove the drive signal to stop; the magnets hold the robot in place.

⚠️ **Safety:** the board generates up to 30 V and the magnets are strong N52 magnets —
take care with the boost converter output and avoid pinching fingers between the
magnets and the steel plate. Instant adhesive is used during assembly.
